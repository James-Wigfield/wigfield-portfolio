The duration of the exam is: **2 hours**.

It is a **closed book exam**.

**You are permitted to bring in**

- 1 UWA approved calculator with sticker.

Details about the exam:

- The exam paper contains 4 questions. Each question is worth 20 marks. The total mark is **80**. **You should attempt all the questions.** The exam contributes **50%** to the total assessment.
- The exam paper covers **all the topics (1 to 9), including lab exericises and the two projects. Sections in the textbook that are not covered in the lecture notes won't be examined.**
- The questions will be **similar (in format) as those in the mid-semester test.**
- Each question will consist of parts and sometimes subparts. Below each part, space is reserved for you to write down your answers. You should see the mark for each part (or each subpart). Use the mark given to each part as a guide to assess how long/deep your answer should be. Words such as "briefly explain" or "explain" also give you an idea how long/deep your answer is expected to be.
- The amount of space reserved below each part is usually sufficient for normal hand-writing. If you tend to write large letters, then use the two extra pages at the end of the exam paper to continue your answers -- make sure that you put down the question and part numbers for your answers. If you still run out of space for writing, then you can request an answer booklet. Make sure you put down your name and student number on the answer booklet also.
- Most parts that are worth more marks usually have more spaces reserved for them. However, sometimes extra spaces are reserved for short questions when there is not enough space to fit the next part of the question on the same page.
- There won't any large block of Python coding in any questions. Instead, you are likely to be given a block of code and you need to draw a diagram or give explanation.

- demonstrate efficacy in applying deep neural networks on structured and unstructured data
- apply sequence processing methods to time-series data
- apply generative models for data generation
- apply generative adversarial networks to learn data distribution
